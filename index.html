How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of offset pegs, with each row wider than the one above it. A ball dropped at the top has an equal chance of moving left or right at every peg it touches, then repeats that same random split all the way down. When the final row is complete, it lands in one of the bottom pockets — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because every step is a simple fifty-fifty turn, most balls settle near the middle and only a few make it to the far edges. Mathematically, the setup is a Galton board and the resulting shape is a binomial distribution. The payout table mirrors that curve: the centre pays less often and the edge pockets pay more because they are harder to reach.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is set to match its chance of landing there, then trimmed slightly for the house. When the payout on each pocket is weighed against its probability, the total comes in a little below one; that difference is the operator margin. Most published versions of the game sit in the roughly 97% to 99% RTP range, depending on the studio and the exact settings chosen.

A good rule to keep in mind: no row count or risk setting changes the long-term maths in your favour. The switches change variance, not the final average, so they alter the ride rather than the destination.

Licensed versions use certified random number generators for each deflection, and provably fair builds publish seed data so results can be checked after the round ends. What none of them can do is help you steer the ball toward a pocket you want.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you check afterwards that the path was fixed before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes rise while their chance falls. The overall return stays essentially the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value in rare edge hits. Choose the one that fits your budget and tolerance for swings.
